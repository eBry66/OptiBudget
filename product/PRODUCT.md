# OptiBudget

## Why this exists

Most households do not manage their budget with a tool built for the purpose.
They use whatever their bank provides, a spreadsheet, or nothing. Each of those
can show what was spent last month. None of them makes it possible to hold the
seven fundamentals of a household budget in one place:

1. Monthly fixed expenses, recurring and non-recurring.
2. Non-monthly recurring expenses — property and income taxes, insurance,
   building charges — funded in advance from a dedicated account rather than
   absorbed as a shock when they fall due.
3. A dedicated reserve for unpredictable large expenses: a car accident, a
   serious health problem, a structural repair.
4. A current inventory of the household's financial holdings — savings, bonds,
   stocks and similar.
5. Building charges, tracked and verified rather than paid on trust.
6. Mortgage payments and interest, tracked closely enough to know when
   renegotiating is worthwhile.
7. Expenses grouped by how far they can be reduced, so that a household under
   pressure can see what it could stop paying, what it could pay less of, and
   what it cannot touch.

Without all seven visible together, a household cannot tell whether its budget
is balanced across a full year, cannot know whether it could absorb a large
unexpected expense, and cannot find the room it needs when income falls. Money
is moved between accounts by instinct, and the answer to "are we ahead or
behind" is a feeling rather than a figure.

OptiBudget exists so that a household can answer that question with a number,
and adjust what it sets aside each month on the strength of it.

## Who it is for

The household, as a unit. A household has members, and access to its financial
information is granted by membership of that household and by nothing else. A
person who administers the application for one household has no access to the
financial information of any other. There is no role that can see across
households.

The first user is PapaG: a retired widower absorbing the loss of income that
followed his wife's death, while facing mandatory energy-performance work on his
apartment. His need is immediate and concrete — to know what he can afford, what
he must set aside, and where he can cut.

The second user is a couple with two children, carrying a mortgage and
undertaking major work on their house, including the certifications that work
requires. They arrive later, and mortgage tracking arrives with them.

Both are real households with real money at stake. Neither is a finance
professional.

## How it treats reducible expenses

Every expense category carries a classification stating how far spending in it
can be reduced: stopped entirely, reduced but not stopped, renegotiated, or not
changed at all. A gym subscription can be stopped. Food can be reduced but not
stopped. A tax bill cannot be touched.

That classification arrives with a sensible default, and the household can
change it, because whether spending is avoidable depends on circumstances rather
than on the kind of spending. A car is optional for a household that could live
without one and necessary for a household that could not.

OptiBudget groups expenses by that classification and shows what each one costs
over a year. It does not decide what should be cut, does not rank candidates,
and does not recommend. The household reads the grouping and decides.

## What it is not

- Not an adviser. It does not recommend what to cut, how to reduce tax, or what
  to invest in. It shows the household its own money clearly and stops there.
- Not a scenario or simulation tool. It reports what is, not what would happen
  if.
- Not a currency manager. It reports in euros. Where a bank or a security states
  an amount in another currency it records that currency and says what rate it
  used, but it does not hold money, convert it, or advise on it.
- Not a real estate management tool. It tracks the charges and the mortgage on a
  home the household lives in, not a portfolio of properties.
- Not an investment platform. It records what the household holds. It does not
  buy, sell, or advise.
- Not a banking application. It reads a history the bank has already produced.
  It never moves money.
- Not a shared or social product. Nothing is compared against other households,
  and nothing leaves the household.

## What working looks like

A member of the household can see, at any moment, how the household's money is
divided between the account it spends from, the funds it sets aside for known
future expenses, its reserve against the unexpected, and its longer-term
holdings — and can change what is set aside each month knowing what that change
costs and what it protects.

They can also see, grouped by how far each can be reduced, what the household
spends across a year, and find the expenses they had stopped noticing.

For PapaG specifically: he can look at one screen and know whether this year is
balanced, what the energy-performance work will take from his reserve, how much
he must set aside each month for the rest of it to hold, and what he could stop
paying if it does not.
